On the 5th of June, Afra, a student at KAHM Unity Women's College, sent Gautham a long WhatsApp message. She wanted to suggest a topic for a future session: the paradox of being highly ambitious but seemingly lazy.

On the 17th of August, she published a 1,400-word essay on that exact paradox, under her own name, on her own Substack.

Seventy-three days. The writing itself took about two of them. This post is about the other seventy-one, because that is where most first drafts go to die.

No document, no outline, no plan. She typed what had been sitting in her head for months and hit send. Is it because we're too comfortable? Fear of failure? Overthinking? Lack of clarity?

Afra's problem was never a shortage of ideas. She had plenty. What she did not have was one finished project.

She did not know it, but this message was her shitty first draft. It had a real question, a point of view and her own experience in it. It was rough, and it existed. That is the whole job of a first draft.

So Gautham replied the same day with three things:

1. You just described this better than most people ever manage.
2. Write it. Other students feel exactly this, and they will read it.
3. Publish it on a Substack. That becomes your first finished project.

Did she blog? No. Had she ever thought about it? No. She was a little overwhelmed, and she said yes anyway. Gautham offered to read her drafts.

That was the entire intervention. A finished project got defined, and she would not be doing it alone.

## Draft two, forty-eight hours later

On the 7th of June, this arrived: "this is how my first draft turned out."

She had read editorials, blogs and articles, watched videos, and noted what she agreed and disagreed with. The draft was a list of questions. Are we lazy, or do we not really want it? Is it the daydreaming that excites us, and the doing that doesn't? Typos, half sentences, no structure.

Still shitty. That is the point. A WhatsApp message became a working draft in two days because she was allowed to make it bad.

Two days later she moved it into a Google Doc. And then the draft sat there. By the 17th of June, her own words were "I've been procrastinating it."

## Where most projects actually die

Most advice about shitty first drafts is about starting: lower the bar, give yourself permission, get it down. All true, and Afra did all of it.

What the advice skips is the stretch right after. The draft exists, the excitement of the idea has worn off, and the work left is slow and unglamorous: restructuring, rewriting, deciding what to cut. Nobody is waiting for it. Nothing breaks if you leave it for a week, and then another.

Afra ended up writing about this very trap in her essay. Imagining a finished project gives you a satisfaction that feels like progress, so your brain collects the reward without the work. Her own draft was living out the paradox it described.

Discipline did not get it moving again, and neither did a deadline. People did.

## What got it moving: people, on a schedule

On the 21st of June, Afra and Gautham met in person at the TinkerHub graduation. A conversation in the same room brought the push back.

On the 1st of July, she joined our Storytellers group. On the 9th, the group started meeting every Wednesday for a Writing Gym, which has since become the [Shitty First Drafts Gym](/real-world-ready/shitty-first-drafts-gym): forty minutes of working, twenty minutes of feedback from a partner, someone else's eyes on your work every week.

On the 14th of July, Afra shared her next draft with the group, with the questions she was stuck on highlighted for feedback.

On the 22nd of July she said she would publish it the next day. It took twenty-six more days. Even with the draft nearly done, finishing was still the hard part, and the weekly rhythm kept bringing her back to it.

On the 17th of August, this landed in the group.

## The ground rules, from Afra's seventy-three days

If you want to use the Shitty First Draft technique on something of your own, here is what this story says works.

**Make it exist before you make it good.** A WhatsApp message counts. A list of bullet points counts. If it is out of your head and somewhere someone else can see it, it is a draft.

**Define what finished means.** "Write about procrastination" is a mood. "Publish one post on Substack" is a project with an end. Afra moved the moment her idea had a finish line.

**Expect the stall after the draft.** Going quiet after draft one is normal. Plan for it and you won't read it as proof that you are lazy.

**Put other people in the loop, on a schedule.** Feedback that arrives every Wednesday beats feedback you have to remember to ask for. The schedule does the remembering for you.

**Fix the big things first.** When feedback comes in, start with purpose, structure and clarity. Leave typos and formatting for last.

## How to use the Gym

The [Shitty First Drafts Gym](/real-world-ready/shitty-first-drafts-gym) is the room Afra found, made available to anyone. It runs every Wednesday, 8 pm to 9 pm, on Zoom.

**Before you start, state your outcome.** In one line: what will you have to show at the forty-minute mark? We added this rule after a session where a newcomer arrived to "work on a project" with a presentation due that Friday. When the feedback round came, there was nothing concrete for his partner to react to. "The first three slides of my presentation" would have given the round something to bite on. If your project is big, pick one slice of it.

**Forty minutes of working.** Everyone works alone, on anything: writing, a portfolio, code, a design, research.

**Twenty minutes of feedback.** You are paired with someone at random. Share a link they can view and comment on, and review each other's work.

**When you give feedback**, help the creator see their work from another angle. You don't need to be an expert. Be specific and explain the why. Useful questions: What grabbed your attention? Where did you lose interest? What confused you? What would you cut, and what do you want more of?

**When you receive feedback**, stay curious. Read everything before acting on any of it, look for patterns across people, ask questions, feel free to disagree, and thank people for their time.

## Your seventy-one days

Afra's first draft took two days. The seventy-one after it are what feedback and company are for. She now has one completed project, and she knows how she finishes things: shitty draft, show someone, fix it, ship it.
